# Science: Food Chains and Ecosystems

**Grade:** 5

**Learning objective:** Identify producers, consumers, and decomposers; explain how energy moves through a simple food chain; and predict one effect when an organism is removed.

## Explanation

Every living thing needs energy to grow, move, and survive. In almost every ecosystem on Earth, this energy begins with the Sun. Sunlight travels to Earth, where special organisms called producers capture it. Producers, such as plants, trees, and algae, use sunlight, water, and air to make their own food through photosynthesis. Animals cannot make their own food, so they are called consumers. Consumers get energy by eating other living things. Some consumers, called herbivores, eat only plants. Other consumers, called carnivores, eat only other animals. Consumers that eat both plants and animals are called omnivores. Finally, when plants and animals die, decomposers go to work. Decomposers, including earthworms, mushrooms, and tiny bacteria, break down dead organic matter and waste. This returns vital nutrients back into the soil so new plants can grow. Together, these organisms form a food chain, which shows how energy moves from one living thing to another. In a food chain diagram, we draw arrows between organisms. An important rule to remember is that the arrow always points toward the organism that is receiving the energy, not what is being eaten. For example, in the chain Clover -> Rabbit -> Fox, the arrow points from the clover to the rabbit because energy moves from the plant into the rabbit's body. If an organism is removed from a food chain, the entire ecosystem changes. Animals that ate that organism may starve or have to search elsewhere for food, while the organisms that were eaten by it might multiply rapidly because they no longer face that predator.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Tracing Energy and Predicting Disruption in a Pond Chain

Look at these four pond organisms: Large Bass (carnivorous fish), Waterweed (green aquatic plant), Osprey (large fish-eating bird), and Mayfly Nymph (small insect that eats plants). First, classify each organism. Second, arrange them into a correct food chain showing the flow of energy. Third, predict what would happen to the mayfly nymph population if all the large bass were removed from the pond.

1. Step 1: Classify each organism based on how it gets food. Waterweed makes its own food using sunlight, so it is a producer. The mayfly nymph eats waterweed, so it is a consumer (herbivore). The large bass eats mayfly nymphs, so it is a consumer (carnivore). The osprey eats bass, so it is also a consumer (carnivore).
2. Step 2: Trace the energy flow starting with the producer. Waterweed gets energy from the Sun and passes it to the mayfly nymph. The mayfly nymph passes energy to the large bass, which passes energy to the osprey. Draw arrows pointing toward the organism receiving the energy: Waterweed -> Mayfly Nymph -> Large Bass -> Osprey.
3. Step 3: Analyze the removal of the large bass. The large bass eats mayfly nymphs. If the bass disappear, mayfly nymphs will have fewer predators hunting them. Therefore, more mayfly nymphs will survive and their population will increase.

**Answer:** Classification: Waterweed is a producer; Mayfly Nymph, Large Bass, and Osprey are consumers. Food chain: Waterweed -> Mayfly Nymph -> Large Bass -> Osprey. Prediction: If large bass are removed, the mayfly nymph population will increase because their main predator is gone.

## Summary

- The Sun is the original source of energy for almost all living things on Earth.
- Producers make their own food, consumers eat other organisms for food, and decomposers recycle dead plants and animals into soil nutrients.
- Food chain arrows always point in the direction that energy moves, toward the organism doing the eating.
- Removing one organism from a food chain disrupts the balance, causing predator populations to decline and prey populations to grow.

## Exercises

1. Read the following list of forest organisms and classify each one as a producer, consumer, or decomposer: Oak Tree, Earthworm, White-tailed Deer, Bracket Fungus, Red-tailed Hawk.
2. A student builds a grassland food chain using grass, a grasshopper, a garter snake, and a meadow frog. Put these organisms in order from start to finish, using arrows to show the correct direction of energy flow.
3. In the food chain Algae -> Mosquito Larva -> Minnow -> Heron, what does the arrow between the mosquito larva and the minnow represent, and why does it point toward the minnow?
4. Consider this simple garden food chain: Tomato Plant -> Aphid (plant-eating insect) -> Ladybug (insect-eating beetle) -> Robin (songbird). If a disease kills all the tomato plants in the garden, predict what will happen to the aphid population and explain why.
5. In the same garden chain (Tomato Plant -> Aphid -> Ladybug -> Robin), predict what would happen to the aphid population if a garden fence kept all robins away, allowing the ladybug population to double.

## Solutions

1. Oak Tree: Producer (uses sunlight to make food); Earthworm: Decomposer (breaks down dead matter and soil debris); White-tailed Deer: Consumer (eats plants); Bracket Fungus: Decomposer (breaks down dead wood); Red-tailed Hawk: Consumer (hunts and eats small animals).
2. Grass -> Grasshopper -> Meadow Frog -> Garter Snake. The grass absorbs sunlight, the grasshopper eats the grass, the frog eats the grasshopper, and the snake eats the frog. The arrows point in the direction energy flows.
3. The arrow represents the flow of energy from the mosquito larva to the minnow. It points toward the minnow because the minnow eats the mosquito larva, meaning the energy moves into the minnow.
4. The aphid population will decrease sharply or die out because aphids depend directly on the tomato plants as their primary source of food and energy.
5. The aphid population would decrease because twice as many ladybugs would be hunting and eating aphids.
